01

The Briefing

Warm-up: five quick questions from this week's case. Wrong answers get a fix-up note, not a red cross and a shrug.

1. A witness wrote sanctury. Which spelling is actually correct?

Fix-up: The trap is a hidden a. Say it slowly: sanc-tu-a-ry. The ending is -ary (like library and February), never -ury.

2. What does the suffix -ment do in displacement?

Fix-up: -ment builds a noun from a verb: displace → displacement, belong → (a sense of) belonging. The "opposite" job belongs to the prefix dis- at the front.

3. Which word means being treated cruelly or unfairly, especially because of your beliefs?

Fix-up: Persecution is cruel, unfair treatment — the reason many refugees flee. Compassion is caring about someone's suffering; resilience is bouncing back from hardship.

4. Which is the correct spelling?

Fix-up: One l, and the ending is -ence (like difference and silence). Say it in four robot beats: re-sil-i-ence.

5. In compassion, which letters make the /sh/ sound?

Fix-up: In compassion the /sh/ is spelt ssi — compassion. Words like action use ti for the same sound. That's exactly why this word is a trap: your ears can't tell you which one to write.

02

Case Notes

Four cards to teach from now — and to come back to when you're studying later.

What is a "tricky part"?

Most of a long word is easy — it sounds exactly how it looks. The tricky part is the small zone where the sound and the spelling don't match, or where a vowel goes quiet. Good spellers don't memorise whole words; they zoom in on the danger zone.

Remember: Find the tricky part, and the rest of the word almost spells itself.

The mumbling vowel (schwa)

In fast speech, unstressed vowels collapse into a lazy "uh" sound — that's the schwa. Your ears hear pers-uh-cution, so they can't tell you whether to write a, e, i, o or u. The fix: say the word like a robot, one clear syllable at a time — per-SE-cu-tion — and the hidden vowel shows itself.

Remember: When a vowel mumbles, robot-talk the word to make it confess.

The /sh/ disguise: -ssion and -tion

The ending sound /shun/ wears two disguises in this week's words: -ssion in compassion and -tion in persecution. Your ears hear the exact same thing — only your eyes can learn which word wears which disguise.

Remember: compassion = double s. persecution = t. Photograph both with your eyes.

The Australian -ise generalisation

Australian English spells these verbs with -ise: organise, recognise, summarise. American books and websites use -ize, so you'll see both — but in your writing, choose s. Phonics twist: that s still sounds like /z/! And one rebel takes -yse: analyse.

Remember: In Australia we organise with an s — even though it sounds like a z. And it's analyse.

05

The -ise Check

Four rapid-fire calls: which spelling belongs in Australian writing?

The detective must ____ her case notes.

Fix-up: Australian English uses -ise: organise. The z version is American.

Did you ____ the pattern in those words?

Fix-up: Recognise — s, not z. It still sounds like /z/ when you say it, which is exactly why your ears can't be trusted here.

Scientists ____ their results before reporting them.

Fix-up: This one's the rebel: analyse takes -yse, not -ise or -ize. It's in the same family as paralyse.

Can you ____ the chapter in three sentences?

Fix-up: Summarise with an s. Quick test for any of these verbs: if you're writing in Australia, reach for s first — then check for the -yse rebels.
